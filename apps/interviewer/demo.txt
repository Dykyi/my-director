Hi, and thanks for joining. Let's start simple. Tell me who you are and what your company does.
Sure. I run a small bicycle repair workshop in the city. I opened it nine years ago in a garage behind my aunt's bakery, with one stand, a box of secondhand tools and a hand-painted sign. These days there are five of us in a proper shop on a busy street. We fix everything from flat tyres to electric bikes, and we still open at seven, because that's when the couriers need us.

What problem were you trying to solve when you started?
Honestly, at first it was my own problem. I spent six years in an office writing reports about delivery routes, and every evening I fixed bikes for neighbours on the pavement outside my flat. Those evenings were the part of the day I actually remembered. And I kept seeing people with a broken bike and nowhere quick and honest to take it. So the shop was about fixing someone's day, not just a wheel.

Who is your typical customer, and how do they find you?
There isn't really one. We see commuters who treat the bike like a kitchen appliance, weekend riders who know every part by name, and parents with three bikes in three sizes. And couriers, our toughest and fairest critics. Most people find us by walking past. They stop at the window to watch someone build a wheel, and a week later they're back with their own bike.

Tell me about a customer you still remember.
Oh, there's one I'll never forget. A man came in and told me, a bit embarrassed, that his bike sounded like a sad duck whenever he climbed a hill. And he was right, it really did. The bottom bracket was dry and loose. I wrote sad duck on the whiteboard, and we still use that phrase for that exact noise. He taught me that half of this job is just listening carefully.

What was the hardest decision you made in the last year?
Probably saying no to money. A few people offered to invest in a second shop, and on paper it made a lot of sense. But a second shop would mean I spend my days managing instead of fixing, and I wasn't sure the quality would survive the split. So I said not yet. It still feels strange to turn down an offer like that, but I think it was right for us.

Tell me about a mistake that taught you something important.
In the first year I tried to sell everything. A supplier told me repairs alone would never pay the rent, so I filled the front of the shop with helmets, lights, bags and clothing. Nobody bought them. I ended up with a wall of stock and a cash problem that kept me awake at night. We sold most of it at a loss. Now we only stock the parts we actually use.

How do you find and train new people?
Every year we take one apprentice, someone who has never worked in a shop. They start by sweeping, sorting bolts and fixing punctures, and after twelve months they can service almost anything that comes through the door. It's the part I'm proudest of, honestly. Two of our former apprentices now run their own workshops across the city, and we send each other customers when one of us is busy.

What does a great week look like for your team?
We run everything from a big whiteboard next to the stands. Every bike gets a line with the owner's name, the job, the promised time and who's working on it. When a line turns red, someone drops what they're doing and helps. So a great week is one where nothing turns red, nobody stays late, and we still have the energy for our free bike care class on Thursday.

What does a normal morning in the shop look like?
I'm usually the first one in. I unlock the door, switch on the lights, look at the whiteboard and make a cup of tea. For a few minutes the shop is completely quiet, and the bikes hang from the ceiling like a row of sleeping bats. Then someone knocks on the glass, usually a courier with a flat tyre, and the day begins. I still love that moment.

Which metric do you watch most closely, and why?
Jobs finished by the promised time. If we tell someone their bike will be ready at four, it should be ready at four. I check that number every week, and it says more about us than the revenue does. When it slips, it usually means we said yes to too many jobs, or that one difficult repair is quietly blocking a stand that someone else needs.

How do you get through the quiet months?
Winter is hard. When it's dark at five and the roads are icy, repair work drops by almost half. The first winter after we moved, I honestly wasn't sure we'd reach spring. Now we plan for it. We offer cheaper winter servicing, we store bikes for people who stay off the roads, and we fix up old bikes to sell in March, when everyone remembers they own one.

How have electric bikes changed your work?
More than anything else in recent years. At first I resisted them, I'll admit that. They were heavy, the parts were hard to find, and the checks needed a laptop. But a lot of those riders hadn't cycled for decades, older people or parents carrying children, and they were so happy to be back on a bike that I couldn't stay grumpy for long. So we learned.

How do you think about waste in the workshop?
A workshop throws away more than you'd think: old tubes, worn tyres, cables, packaging and used oil. Now we send our tubes to a group that turns them into belts and bags, we collect the scrap metal, and we buy oil in big refillable drums. It isn't perfect, and it costs a little more. But it feels wrong to fix clean machines and leave a mess behind.

What would you tell a founder who is just starting out?
Three things. Know your costs to the last coin, because guessing is how good shops close. Hire people who are better than you at something, and then actually let them do it. I hovered over my first mechanic for a whole month, which was silly. And be honest with every customer, even when honesty costs you a job. Those three things have saved us more than once.

Where do you want the company to be in three years?
Not bigger, just better. I'd like a larger workspace, a proper training room and shorter waits. The idea I'm most excited about is a small van with a stand, a compressor and a good set of tools, so we can visit people who can't easily bring a bike to us. Older customers, big families, offices with a fleet. If the spring goes well, we'll try it next year.

Thanks. Last one: what is something people usually get wrong about your industry?
People think a repair shop is about bikes. It's really about people's days. A flat tyre is a small job, but it's also a small emergency. Someone is late for work, or a courier is losing money by the minute. And people think customers want cheap. They don't. When I finally raised my prices and explained why, almost nobody complained. A fair price earns more respect than a cheap one.

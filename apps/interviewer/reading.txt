I opened the workshop nine years ago in a garage behind my aunt's bakery. I had one stand, a box of secondhand tools and a hand-painted sign that leaned against the wall because I was too nervous to drill holes in the brick. Most mornings the whole place smelled of bread and chain oil. People still tell me they miss that smell, and honestly, so do I, a little.

Before the workshop I spent six years in an office, writing reports about delivery routes for a company that moved furniture. The job was fine and the pay was steady. But every evening I came home and fixed bikes for neighbours on the pavement outside my flat, and I noticed that the evenings were the part of the day I actually remembered. At some point that stopped feeling like a hobby and started feeling like a hint.

The first year was mostly flat tyres. I mean that almost literally. I kept a tally on the back of the door, and by December there were more than eleven hundred marks on it. A flat tyre is a small job, but it is also a small emergency. Someone is late for work, or a child is upset, or a courier is losing money by the minute. You learn quickly that you are fixing a day, not a wheel.

Our customers fall into a few loose groups. There are commuters who ride every day and treat the bike like a kitchen appliance. There are weekend riders who know every part by name and love to talk about it. There are parents with three bikes in the family, all of them different sizes. And there are couriers, who are the toughest and fairest critics we have.

Couriers taught me more about this business than any course could. They ride in all weather, they carry heavy bags, and a broken bike means no income that day. So they need speed, honesty and a fair price, in that order. We started opening at seven in the morning because of them. We keep a shelf of the parts they break most often, and we never make them wait behind a slow job.

My first big mistake was trying to sell everything. I filled the front of the shop with helmets, lights, bags, bottles and clothing, because a supplier told me that repairs alone would never pay the rent. Within a year I had a wall of stock that nobody bought and a cash problem that kept me awake at night. We sold most of it at a loss, and I promised myself to stock only what we use.

The second mistake was about pricing. I charged too little for a long time because I was afraid people would leave. What actually happened was that I worked twelve-hour days, earned very little, and rushed jobs that deserved more care. When I finally raised prices and explained why, almost nobody complained. A few regulars said it was about time. People respect a fair price far more than a cheap one.

The third mistake was saying yes to every job. Someone would bring in a bicycle that had sat in a shed for twenty years, with rust in every bearing, and ask us to make it like new for very little money. I would agree, because I hate to disappoint anyone. Then the job would take three days and block a stand. Now we give an honest estimate first, and sometimes the honest answer is no.

I hired my first mechanic in the third year. She had worked for a racing team and was far better at building wheels than I will ever be. For the first month I hovered over her shoulder and checked everything she did, which was silly and a little insulting. One afternoon she handed me a wheel she had just trued and asked me to find anything wrong with it. I couldn't. I stopped hovering after that.

Today there are five of us: two senior mechanics, one apprentice, a part-time person who runs the front desk and the bookings, and me. I still work on bikes three days a week, because if I stop, I will lose touch with what the job feels like. The other two days go to suppliers, the accounts, the schedule and all the small problems that never fit on anyone else's list.

The apprentice program is the part I am proudest of. Every year we take one young person who has never worked in a shop. They start by sweeping, sorting bolts and fixing punctures, and after twelve months they can service almost anything that comes through the door. Two of our former apprentices now run their own workshops across the city. We send each other customers when one of us is too busy.

We run the shop with a big whiteboard next to the stands. Every bike gets a line with the owner's name, the job, the promised time and the person working on it. It sounds old-fashioned, and we tried software twice, but the board wins because everyone can see it from anywhere in the room. When a line turns red, someone drops what they are doing and helps. Nobody has to ask twice.

A lot of the job is listening. People describe a sound, a click or a creak, and they are usually a bit embarrassed about how they describe it. One man told me his bike sounded like a sad duck whenever he climbed a hill. He was right, it did. The bottom bracket was dry and loose. I wrote sad duck on the board, and we still use that phrase for that particular noise.

We also try to teach. Every second Thursday we run a free evening class on basic care: how to fix a puncture, how to adjust brakes, how to clean and oil a chain. Some people worry that this takes work away from us, but it does the opposite. People who understand their bikes trust us more, ride more often and come back for the jobs they cannot do at home.

Four years ago we moved out of the garage into a proper shop on a busy street. It was the most frightening decision I have made. The rent tripled overnight, and for the first two months the numbers looked terrible. Then the street started to notice us. People walking past would stop to watch someone build a wheel in the window, and many of them came back later with a bike.

There have been hard winters. Fewer people ride when it is dark at five and the roads are icy, and repair work drops by almost half. The first winter after the move, I wasn't sure we would reach spring. We survived by offering cheaper winter servicing, storing bikes for people who stay off the roads in the cold, and fixing up old bikes to sell in March, when everyone remembers they own one.

Electric bikes changed the work more than anything else in recent years. At first I resisted them. They were heavy, the parts were hard to find and the checks needed a laptop. But many of the riders had not cycled for decades, older customers or parents carrying children, and they were so happy to be back on a bicycle that I couldn't stay grumpy for long. So we learned.

We think a lot about waste. A workshop throws away a surprising amount: old tubes, worn tyres, cables, packaging and used oil. We now send tubes to a group that turns them into belts and bags, we collect scrap metal, and we buy oil in large refillable drums. It is not perfect, and it costs a little more, but it feels wrong to fix clean machines and leave a mess behind.

People sometimes ask me whether the shop is a success. I am never quite sure how to answer. We are not rich. The margins are thin, and a bad month still worries me. But we pay everyone fairly, some customers have trusted us for nine years, and on most days the work is good. If success means wanting to come back tomorrow, then yes, I think we are doing fine.

As for plans, the obvious next step would be a second shop, and a few people have offered to invest in one. I keep saying not yet. A second shop means I would spend my days managing instead of fixing, and I am not sure the quality would survive the split. I would rather make this one better, with a bigger workspace, a proper training room and shorter waits.

The idea I am most excited about is a small van. Many of our customers cannot easily bring a broken bike to us: older people, families with several children, offices with a fleet of bikes for their staff. A van with a stand, a compressor and a good set of tools could visit them instead. We have priced it out twice. If the spring goes well, we will try it next year.

If someone asked me for advice about starting a workshop like this, I would say three things. Know your costs to the last coin, because guessing is how good shops close. Hire people who are better than you at something, and then let them do it. And be honest with every customer, even when honesty costs you a job. Those three things have saved us more than once.

Most days I still arrive before everyone else. I unlock the door, switch on the lights, look at the whiteboard and make a cup of tea. For a few minutes the shop is completely quiet, and the bikes hang from the ceiling like a row of sleeping bats. Then the first customer knocks on the glass, usually a courier with a flat tyre, and the day begins again.
